Reading the schedule
  • Early EMIs are mostly interest because interest is charged on a large balance.
  • The principal share grows every month as the balance falls.
  • The last EMI can be a few rupees off due to rounding at the bank.
When the rate changes
  • Floating-rate loans follow the lender's benchmark, so the rate here is a snapshot.
  • Banks usually stretch the tenure when rates rise and keep the EMI the same.
  • Try a rate 1% higher to see how much room your budget has.
Not included
  • Processing fees and insurance bundled with the loan.
  • Pre-EMI interest on under-construction homes.
  • Tax deductions, which are on the Prepay tab.

How the two plans are compared
  • Both plans spend the same cash each month: the original EMI plus the planned prepayment.
  • Prepay: once the EMI drops or the loan ends, the freed-up money is invested.
  • Invest: the prepayment money is invested and the loan runs its full term.
The break-even return
  • Without the tax deduction it equals the loan rate: prepaying is a guaranteed return at that rate.
  • With the deduction the loan costs less after tax, so investing wins at a lower return.
  • Use a return after tax, and remember investments can fall while a prepayment can't.
Before you prepay
  • Keep an emergency fund; money in a loan is hard to get back.
  • Floating-rate home loans to individuals usually carry no prepayment charge; check yours.
  • Reduce tenure saves more interest than reduce EMI for the same prepayment.
The ₹2 lakh interest deduction · old regime only
The rule
  • Interest on a loan for a self-occupied home is deductible up to ₹2,00,000 a financial year.
  • Old regime only. The new regime gives no deduction for a self-occupied home.
  • Section 24(b) of the 1961 Act became section 22 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 from 1 April 2026, with the same limit.
How this page uses it
  • Tax saved = slab × 1.04 (cess) × the lower of that year's interest and ₹2 lakh.
  • Savings are invested at the end of each financial year (March) in both plans.
  • Not modelled: surcharge, the principal deduction under 80C, joint borrowers and let-out homes.

How it works
  • The renter invests the down payment and stamp duty on day one.
  • Each month both spend the same: whoever pays less (rent vs EMI + upkeep) invests the gap.
  • Buyer's net worth = home value − loan left + anything they invested.
What moves the answer most
  • Rent as a share of price: Indian rents are often 2–4% of the price a year.
  • Home price growth versus the return on investments.
  • How long you stay: stamp duty is a cost you recover only over time.
Left out
  • Selling costs (brokerage) and tax on the gain when you sell.
  • Home loan tax deductions and moving costs.
  • Rent deposits, interiors and the value of stability.
Stamp duty and registration · varies by state
  • Stamp duty is set by each state, usually 4–7% of the higher of the price and the government's circle rate; many states charge women buyers less.
  • Registration is often 1%, capped in some states. Sources disagree on several state rates, so check your state's registration portal.
  • Source used for the 7% default: BankBazaar state-wise list, checked Nov 2026.

About this calculator

The arithmetic
  • EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1), with r = annual rate ÷ 12 and n = months.
  • Each month interest = balance × r; the rest of the EMI repays principal.
  • Check: ₹50,00,000 at 8.5% for 20 years gives an EMI of ₹43,391.
Conventions
  • All returns compound monthly at the annual rate ÷ 12, the same way the loan does.
  • Investments are made at month end, after that month's EMI.
  • Prepayments are applied right after the EMI they follow.
Estimate, not advice
  • These are estimates, not financial or tax advice.
  • Your lender's schedule is final; they may round daily or use a different day count.
  • Everything runs in your browser. Nothing you type is sent anywhere.
