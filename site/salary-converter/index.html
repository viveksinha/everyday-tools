Reading the result
  • "Same standard of living" is the salary that buys the same everyday basket of goods and services there.
  • The exchange-rate figure is what you'd get if you converted the money at a bank.
  • The gap between them is how much cheaper or dearer everyday life is.
What it leaves out
  • Taxes and social security. Compare take-home pay if you can.
  • City versus country. Mumbai, London or San Francisco cost well above their national average.
  • Rent, school fees and healthcare can swing far more than the average basket.
Imported things don't get cheaper
  • Phones, laptops, cars and flights cost about the same everywhere, so use the exchange-rate figure for those.
  • Savings you'll move abroad are also worth the exchange-rate amount, not the PPP one.

How it works

Purchasing power parity (PPP)
  • Statisticians price the same basket of goods and services in every country.
  • The PPP factor is how many units of local currency buy what 1 US dollar buys in the US.
  • This page uses the household-spending basket ("private consumption"), which suits salaries better than the whole-economy one.
The arithmetic
  • Same standard of living = amount ÷ PPP of home country × PPP of the other country.
  • Exchange rate = amount ÷ home rate to the dollar × other country's rate.
  • Prices vs home = (PPP ÷ exchange rate) there, divided by the same at home.
About the data
  • World Bank figures are annual and lag a year or so; the table shows each country's year.
  • Exchange rates are that year's average, not today's rate.
  • Some countries are missing where the World Bank has no recent PPP figure.
